# Chinese Human-Feeling Lyrics

Use this reference for Chinese lyric creation, rewriting, audit, and optimization. Its goal is not to disguise machine output through random imperfection. Its goal is to write a believable narrator whose words arise from a specific moment, relationship, and pressure.

## Protect the boundaries

- Preserve user facts and protected phrases. Never invent a personal memory, private event, quotation, injury, promise, or relationship detail and present it as true.
- Permit invented detail only when the work is clearly fictional, hypothetical, or the user has authorized creative filling. Keep fictional details internally consistent.
- Do not imitate a named living writer's, lyricist's, or creator's identity, signature phrases, fixed persona, or highly recognizable voice. Translate references into general mechanisms.
- Do not equate “human” with slang, typos, profanity, ellipses, or disorder. Use irregularity only when it expresses character, pressure, timing, or subtext.
- Keep meaning, singability, and the user's voice above any anti-AI heuristic.

## Establish source truth and narrator

Before writing, identify:

- Who is speaking, to whom, and at what distance in time.
- What the speaker knows, does not know, and refuses to say directly.
- Where the body is and what it is doing.
- Which facts are supplied, which details are fictional, and which areas must remain unspecified.
- One linguistic habit appropriate to this narrator: clipped replies, careful understatement, concrete counting, a recurring question, or another restrained pattern.

Use a particular speaking position, not a generic “lyrical I.” A convincing voice can contradict itself, notice the wrong detail first, avoid the central word, or reveal feeling through behavior. Keep those choices coherent rather than random.

## Convert emotion into evidence

Prefer evidence over explanation:

```text
abstract label → body, action, object, timing, or failed speech
“我很舍不得” → 门关了，我还握着那把没用的伞
“我终于释怀” → 我把你的地址，从外卖备注里删掉
```

The examples illustrate transformation only; do not reuse them as stock lyrics.

For every major emotion, seek one of:

- A bodily reaction: breath held, hand withdrawn, throat tightening, sleep interrupted.
- A small action: deleting, folding, reheating, waiting, turning an object over.
- A social mismatch: saying “没事” while behaving otherwise.
- A time trace: something still present days later, or a habit that has quietly changed.
- An object with relational history: not a decorative prop, but something affected by the relationship.

Do not explain every image. Leave a recoverable gap for the listener, but keep the basic situation intelligible on first hearing.

## Write natural Chinese lyric speech

- Start from natural spoken word order, then compress for melody. Do not invert syntax only to force rhyme.
- Mix line lengths within a controlled melodic group. Avoid four consecutive lines with identical grammar unless deliberate parallelism powers the hook.
- Use complete phrases, short fragments, omitted subjects, questions, or self-correction where a person would naturally pause.
- Prefer verbs and relational nouns over adjective piles. One precise verb often carries more life than three emotional adjectives.
- Let the Chorus simplify. The most human line may also be the plainest line.
- Use colloquial language from the narrator's world, not a generic list of trendy expressions.
- Retain a user's unusual but intelligible phrase when it carries identity; repair only what blocks meaning, singing, or the agreed tone.

Read each section aloud internally. If it sounds like an essay broken into lines, rebuild the syntax around breath, stress, recurrence, and melodic grouping.

## Progress details and callbacks

Treat details as actions in the song, not decorations:

1. Introduce an ordinary object or gesture in Verse 1.
2. Change its context, owner, use, or emotional weight in Verse 2.
3. Recall it briefly in the Final Chorus or Outro only if the return changes what it means.

Make Verse 2 produce consequence. It should reveal a later time, missing person, altered routine, contradictory memory, or decision—not paraphrase Verse 1 with new rhymes.

Use contrast at human scale: what was said versus done, public composure versus private behavior, a large feeling beside a trivial task. Avoid automatic “small scene → universal truth” elevation unless the user's concept genuinely calls for it.

## Repair generic lyric language

Treat a line as suspect when it could move unchanged into hundreds of unrelated songs. Common warning signs include:

- Unsupported emotional declarations.
- Interchangeable cosmic or inspirational imagery.
- Abstract noun clusters with no person or action.
- Perfectly balanced parallel sentences that add no new information.
- A slogan that summarizes the theme but reveals no speaker.
- Logical connectors that sound like an explanation or report.
- Every line resolving neatly, leaving no subtext or pressure.

Do not ban words mechanically. Words such as 星光、远方、梦想、翅膀、海、时光、人海、命运、温柔 can work when tied to a concrete relationship, action, setting, or changed meaning. Repair their function, not merely their vocabulary.

Use this replacement sequence:

```text
generic claim
→ identify the exact speaker and moment
→ find truthful evidence already available
→ choose one action or object
→ rewrite in natural word order
→ compress for breath and melody
→ recheck rhyme without weakening the detail
```

## Run the four-pass optimization

### Pass 1 — Truth and voice

- Are facts, relationships, viewpoint, addressee, and edit authority preserved?
- Is fictional invention clearly within scope?
- Does the diction belong to one narrator, or does the voice change to chase rhymes?

### Pass 2 — Scene and emotional temperature

- Can the listener locate at least one moment and action?
- Does each major emotion have observable evidence?
- Does Verse 2 add consequence or changed understanding?
- Is any “big truth” earned by the preceding detail?

### Pass 3 — Speech, prosody, and song function

- Does the Chinese word order sound natural aloud?
- Are fragments, pauses, repetitions, and asymmetries intentional?
- Are line lengths, stresses, vowels, rhyme, and breath compatible with the section?
- Is the Chorus easier to retain than the Verse?

### Pass 4 — Distinctiveness and afterimage

- Which line could only belong to this narrator and this song?
- Which line still sounds interchangeable or AI-polished?
- Has over-editing removed an idiosyncratic but useful phrase?
- Does the ending leave a concrete afterimage or transform an earlier detail?

Repair only the failed layer and rerun the affected musical checks. Do not flatten an entire lyric merely to make every line equally polished.
